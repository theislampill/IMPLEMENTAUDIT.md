## Multi-agent systems: mechanisms, conditions and collective failure

## Status and principal result

**Author candidate EMAS-RQ1, 11 November 2026. Independent fidelity and adversarial reviews are pending.** This is a neutral scholarly requalification of the complete inherited Multi-Agent Systems study. It preserves all 76 property identities, 56 source identities, 61 composition-relation identities, nine original packet members and every original neutral conflict statement. The surviving account of multi-agent systems is plural and conditional. Interaction matters when independently situated participants affect one another’s feasible actions, information, incentives, commitments or outcomes. Merely dividing work into named agents does not establish this interdependence, solve it, or justify its costs. Distributed problem solving, intentional teamwork, public commitments, strategic exchange, organisational norms, distributed optimisation, argumentation and multi-agent learning supply different mechanisms for different problems. They do not form a compulsory sequence, and their assumptions cannot safely be combined by taking their union. [EMAS-001–004, 007–012, 030–038, 043–054; native report “Research judgement” and “Reconstructing the field without a linear progress story”; selected property ledger.]

The requalification makes three scientific corrections. First, EMAS-005 incorrectly made independent failures a prerequisite of a resilience claim. Bounded-fault guarantees can instead tolerate arbitrary, correlated behaviour within a stated fault bound. The corrected claim requires an adequate fault/dependency model and protection mechanism; decentralisation alone supplies neither. Second, the two-page ARMOR demonstration was assigned comparative-efficacy roles in eight property records and an unsupported truthful-reporting role in EMAS-031. Those roles are removed while its legitimate deployment evidence remains. Third, EMAS-075 now includes the FT-DCOP source’s operative separation between computing agents and actors that choose final assignments: a faulty computer is not allowed to override the resulting physical decision. This boundary materially limits the guarantee. [EMAS-S042, complete pp.1884–1885; EMAS-S051, §4.2 and §5; `NATIVE_FIELD_CHANGES.json`, RQ-C01–03.]

## 1. Several origins, several objects of explanation

The early distributed problem-solving problem was practical: several processors possess different knowledge or capabilities, and a task must find a suitable recipient. Smith’s Contract Net protocol organises task announcements, bids and awards through manager and contractor roles that can change with the task. Its opening model is cooperative and assumes supporting communication services. It does not derive honest strategic bidding, guaranteed execution or globally optimal assignment from the vocabulary of a contract. Durfee and Lesser’s Partial Global Planning, or PGP, studies a related but different problem: concurrent local plans interact while participants hold incomplete or inconsistent global views. Planning, messaging and execution proceed together. Its simulation comparisons expose the cost of planning and communication as well as their potential benefit. A result measured in knowledge-source executions is not automatically a wall-clock improvement in an unrelated system. [EMAS-S004, pp.1–3; EMAS-S006, pp.1–2,7–8.]

There is documentary contact between these traditions: PGP explicitly cites Contract Net and functionally accurate/cooperative distributed problem solving. That supports the native shared-ancestry account, without proving that one algorithm was derived from one earlier implementation. Similarly, SharedPlans cites a wider distributed-AI literature, but resemblance between partial plans does not establish direct PGP descent. The requalification therefore rejects an initially considered ancestry correction: the original relationship already disclaims direct derivation. The meaning of an edge is limited by its documentary basis. [`GENEALOGY_REQUALIFICATION.json`, EMAS-GE002; native relation EMAS-REL-016; EMAS-S008, introduction and bibliography.]

Intentional teamwork asks why parallel individual intentions fail to explain joint persistence. Cohen and Levesque’s joint intentions make continued participation and communication about changed circumstances part of the undertaking. Grosz and Kraus’s SharedPlans allows recipes and allocations to remain incomplete while participants intend that others succeed and resolve missing pieces. These are different explanatory accounts, not successive versions of one protocol. STEAM explicitly combines selected joint-intention and SharedPlans ideas; its account of team monitoring, repair and communication cost is a documented hybridisation. Full mutual belief remains a demanding idealisation, especially when communication or observation is uncertain. Helpful intentions also face competing obligations and resource costs that the SharedPlans paper acknowledges without fully solving. [EMAS-S007, pp.1,3–5; EMAS-S008, pp.1–2,51–53; EMAS-S009, §3.3 and pp.11–12.]

Communication and institutions developed another strand. KQML and FIPA distinguish communicative acts, message content and intended effects. Singh’s critique argues that private sincerity or belief cannot be a generally observable compliance criterion for opaque, independently controlled agents. Public social commitments choose a different semantic object: who is obligated to whom, under which conditions, and how interaction changes that obligation. This critique does not disprove the usefulness of mental-state models inside an inspectable cooperative architecture. It identifies a boundary at which their private semantics cease to provide a public test. Information protocols such as BSPL then analyse information causality and legal local enactment without equating those objects with mental intentions or fulfilled contracts. [EMAS-S012–016; fresh EMAS-S013, pp.2–4; EMAS-S016, pp.1,3–4; EMAS-S044, pp.1,5.]

The economic and social-choice foundations predate much of this engineering. Gibbard’s 1973 result and Myerson and Satterthwaite’s 1983 bilateral-trade theorem were not invented by multi-agent research. They constrain selected formal models of strategic reporting and allocation. Organisations such as Gaia and MOISE+ address roles, responsibilities, goals and norms; distributed constraint reasoning addresses assignment feasibility and objective quality; argumentation provides semantics for conflicting reasons. Deep multi-agent learning later adds policy adaptation, training/execution information differences and partner-dependent conventions. The umbrella term “multi-agent systems” makes these problems comparable, but does not make their assumptions interchangeable. [EMAS-S018–025, 026–034; the complete dated 25-node, 25-edge genealogy remains in the selected research-coverage register.]

## 2. Messages change different things

A delivered string can fail to change any relevant decision. The recipient may not understand its referent, may reject the request, may lack authority or capability, or may accept an obligation but never perform it. Conversely, some protocols define an obligation directly through a particular message transition. The useful distinctions are delivery, interpretation, commitment and external consequence; they need not be four separate handshakes. This prevents both overclaiming from a receipt and adding ceremonial acknowledgements that the selected semantics do not need. [EMAS-007–016; EMAS-S004; EMAS-S016; newly inspected EMAS-S053.]

The final FIPA Contract Net interaction specification illustrates the latter point. Its proposal is binding if the initiator accepts it, rather than merely inviting another acceptance by the proposer. The protocol also distinguishes successful completion from failure and associates conversations and replies with identifiers and deadlines. These are normative rules of the specified interaction, not a guarantee that an arbitrary participant obeys them or that a completion message reports the physical truth. The specification’s exceptions discuss cancellation and leave effects on the real world, asynchrony and nested protocol termination outside parts of its treatment. A later implementation needs correspondence evidence for those boundaries. [EMAS-S053, SC00029H, Standard dated 2002-12-03, compilation pp.24–32; cover and flow pages visually inspected.]

This new access repairs an access limitation, not a current-runtime conclusion. The original failed retrieval remains historical. The final communicative-act library SC00037J still was not successfully read; the earlier inspected XC00037H experimental document is another version, and Contract Net SC00029H is another work. EMAS-013 remains unresolved because one readable historical standard cannot establish current version equivalence, conformance, portability or interoperability. The next discriminator is an exact final-profile comparison plus tests of the actual implementations whose compatibility is claimed. [EMAS-S014–015,053; EMAS-K004; `EXTERNAL_SOURCE_DEPENDENCIES.json`.]

BSPL supplies a second boundary. Its §3.5 liveness condition makes legal completion possible from reachable enactments; it does not compel an agent to send a message eventually. A legal trace that stops forever is therefore not refuted merely by showing the model has a possible continuation. Similarly, the inspected commitment-alignment result assumes identifiable communication with reliable, ordered point-to-point delivery and evaluates suitable quiescent or integral observations. If the creditor infers a commitment, the debtor must infer the corresponding commitment under those conditions. That is different from everyone sharing the same instantaneous view, and still different from the promised service being performed. [EMAS-S016, §3.5; EMAS-S044, assumptions A1–A4 and alignment definition.]

These distinctions explain why conversation causality, public effects and outcome observation survive while performative naming alone is ceremonial. A small authorised scheduler may satisfy the relevant dependency with one request and a measured result. A richer public protocol becomes useful when distinct principals need observable obligations, refusal, delegation, disputes or exception handling. Neither solution follows just from calling the participants agents. [EMAS-010–014,025–026,069; configuration EMAS-CFG01.]

## 3. Coordination is repairable dependence, with a cost

The point of a joint plan is to align consequential contributions. A team whose participants know only their own assignments may fail when one task becomes impossible and the others continue expending resources. Joint-intention theories preserve a duty to communicate relevant discoveries; SharedPlans represents unfinished recipes and allocations; PGP exchanges enough plan information to reason about interactions; operational teamwork adds monitoring and repair. These mechanisms differ in how much state they assume is available and how they balance local execution against global coordination. [EMAS-018,020–028; EMAS-S006–009.]

The operative questions are concrete. Which actor knows a task is blocked? Who can reassign it? Does a departing participant remain responsible until another accepts? How does a changed plan affect a commitment already visible to outsiders? Resource budgets, authority and capabilities must be supplied by the application; a role label supplies none of them. The native records retain decommitment and notification conditions, detection of abandoned obligations, and the need for progress rather than merely legal local transitions. An impossible goal cannot be repaired by endless reorganisation or repeated messages. [EMAS-004,016,020–021,027,039–040,059–063; EMAS-S011,044,046,050.]

Communication is valuable when it changes a consequential choice or prevents avoidable joint failure. It consumes time, bandwidth, computation and human attention. PGP’s comparisons already make this tension visible; later teamwork explicitly reasons about communication cost. The contemporary alem study reports a targeted ablation in which removing communication harms selected coordination tasks, but that does not imply that every extra message helps. The small human-explanation study likewise does not establish a universal benefit of explaining every subtask. Both maximal messaging and maximal silence can be inadequate. [EMAS-S006, pp.7–8; EMAS-S009, pp.11–12; EMAS-S038, §4.2.2; EMAS-S054, §§2–3; EMAS-K019,024.]

The cheap path is therefore part of the theory. If a stable allocation, direct scheduler, posted offer or ordinary service boundary satisfies the requirement with less uncertainty and cost, the coordination machinery should be reduced or omitted. This is a comparative proposition to assess under the actual task, not a presumption that centralisation is always cheaper or that agent autonomy has no value. [EMAS-001,019,022,062,065,073; EMAS-CFG01–04.]

## 4. Strategic stability, truthful reports and social value are separate

Game models specify players, actions, information and payoffs before a solution concept says anything. A stable outcome under those assumptions may be inefficient, unequal, harmful to outsiders or institutionally unacceptable. A mechanism may discourage a selected misreport while failing to implement its assumed transfers. Cooperative cost estimates need not be strategic reports at all. Consequently, the native incentive-alignment, truthful-reporting, welfare, collusion and contract-search properties retain distinct predicates. [EMAS-003,017,030–035; EMAS-S023–025,040,045–046.]

Myerson and Satterthwaite’s result makes the importance of premises especially clear. The inspected model has two risk-neutral traders with private values independently drawn from continuous distributions with positive densities over overlapping supports; it combines Bayesian incentive compatibility, interim individual rationality and a budget constraint with efficient trade. The impossibility is about their joint attainability in that model. The paper itself discusses a discrete-support exception, so replacing its distributional premises with the phrase “private information” would overstate the result. The theorem neither prohibits every useful market nor proves that a particular deployed mechanism is truthful. [EMAS-S024, pp.1–4,7–9; formal register and EMAS-031–032.]

Gibbard’s manipulation result similarly concerns the specified deterministic preference domain and at least three possible outcomes, with a dictatorship condition in its formal conclusion. Restrictions on preferences, outcome domains or randomisation require their own analysis. The appropriate use of an impossibility theorem is to identify which desired combination is unavailable under stated premises, then make an explicit design tradeoff. It is not a generic objection to all collective decision procedures. [EMAS-S025, pp.1–3; EMAS-047.]

ARMOR demonstrates a different contribution. The inspected two-page report describes an operational security-scheduling aid based on a Stackelberg model, with human-supplied constraints and possible overrides. It provides evidence that the system was built and used. It does not compare crime or security outcomes against a controlled counterfactual, and it does not implement a truthful type-reporting mechanism. The old source table and empirical register already warned against overreading the deployment; the specific error was its promotion in property-level evidence partitions. EMAS-031 therefore loses that field-case citation and has no comparative payoff evidence from it. Its theoretical basis and assumption-sensitive disposition remain. Seven other properties retain ARMOR’s legitimate field-demonstration role. [EMAS-S042, pp.1884–1885; EMP014; exact corrections RQ-C02.]

The later algorithmic-collusion case remains an adverse boundary in the native corpus. Stability in a restricted repeated-pricing meta-game cannot be treated as social legitimacy, and its finite strategy menu does not establish all possible market behaviour. Neither that simulation nor ARMOR justifies collapsing strategic reasoning into a universal recommendation for auctions, bargaining or game-theoretic control. [EMAS-S040, inherited source scope; EMAS-033–035,055; EMAS-K009.]

## 5. Organisations describe powers they do not automatically possess

Roles, missions and obligations can make a collective easier to understand and change. The native organisation records distinguish structural relations, functional goals and deontic constraints. Those distinctions remain useful because a role chart alone does not make a service available, grant permission, create jurisdiction or cause compliance. Gaia’s original scope and MOISE+’s reorganisation examples remain bounded by the kinds of participants and changes they actually model. [EMAS-036,039–042; EMAS-S018–019,050, inherited primary scope.]

Normative programs separate preventing a prohibited action from detecting and sanctioning it after it occurs. Regimentation can remove an action from the available transition system; enforcement depends on observing a violation and applying a consequence with real authority and effect. Voluntary compliance is another behavioural mechanism. If the institution cannot observe an external act, or the participant is outside its powers, an internal normative declaration does not solve the problem. These are failure conditions, not reasons to discard every norm-based architecture. [EMAS-S020, pp.2–3; EMAS-S021, inherited enforceability scope; EMAS-037–038,041.]

The 2026 norm-aware BDI extended abstract adds norm-related alternatives to goal-plan reasoning. Its Jason example includes the possibility of deliberately violating a norm after considering its associated value or sanction. That is evidence of norm-sensitive deliberation, not automatic compliance. The three-page work leaves larger-scale performance and further extensions for later evaluation; the cited longer report was not freshly read here. The correct status remains domain specific, with implementation and running-example evidence rather than a general legitimacy or scalability result. [EMAS-S052, complete extended abstract; EMAS-076; EMAS-K022.]

Organisational change must also preserve relevant existing obligations. Replacing a role or learning a new policy can invalidate the expectations of partners who were never informed. A system needs an explicit account of what continues, what is delegated, and what can be withdrawn. There are contexts in which discretion and exceptions are necessary, and others in which preventing a consequential act is appropriate. The native tension between enforcement and discretion remains unresolved by terminology alone. [EMAS-021,026,040,072,074,076; EMAS-T007,010.]

## 6. Distributed computation and learning keep different guarantees

Distributed constraint satisfaction asks for feasible assignments; optimisation adds an objective. A mathematically valid assignment still requires correct problem modelling and a path to execution. DPOP’s pseudo-tree approach exchanges a bounded number of messages, but utility-message size can grow exponentially with induced width. Counting messages alone therefore conceals a material cost. Privacy is also protocol-relative: keeping a utility table local does not prove that its contents cannot be inferred from exchanged summaries or final decisions. The native distinction among feasibility, quality, leakage and real-world payoff remains. [EMAS-S028, Theorem 1 and pp.4–5; EMAS-S026–030 at inherited scope; EMAS-043–045,065,069.]

Abstract argumentation addresses acceptance among arguments and attacks. A stable extension can fail to exist; a self-attacking argument supplies a finite witness. Other semantics answer different questions. Even when a chosen extension exists, the graph alone does not establish the truth of its premises. The inspected dialogue adaptation explicitly cites Dung and adds preference-sensitive argumentation and dialogue moves. Its winning-tree reasoning is not a guarantee that every live exchange reaches a true agreement, and its stated scope does not already cover arbitrary negotiation or multiparty dialogue. The protocol survey likewise distinguishes public commitment stores from private belief. [EMAS-S031, pp.1,3–9; EMAS-S055, pp.1,3,7; EMAS-S056, pp.1,4–6,16–17; EMAS-046–049.]

Learning adds a different source of movement: each learner’s changing policy changes other learners’ effective environment. Centralised training with decentralised execution is one response, not the definition of multi-agent learning. It assumes that training can obtain joint information that execution need not receive. QMIX further chooses a monotonic factorisation of the joint action-value function. This is a sufficient structure for the relevant decentralised greedy selection, not a necessary property of every useful joint value function. A nonmonotonic payoff table can lie outside the representable class without making decentralised control impossible in general. [EMAS-S032, pp.1–2,4; EMAS-S033, pp.1–4; EMAS-050–053.]

Good self-play can rely on conventions that fail with another partner. Other-Play explicitly addresses zero-shot coordination under known game symmetries and distinguishes that setting from ad-hoc teamwork and external semantic cues. The separately added November 2026 preprint compares self-play and cross-play among LLM agents, including independently generated code policies and different underlying models. Its simple coordination tasks expose both incompatibility and under-coordination: labels that make a focal convention obvious to a person do not guarantee shared behaviour. The paper does not causally isolate every failure factor; its text-agent evaluation is narrower than its code-agent evaluation, and those modes are not quantitatively interchangeable. It strengthens the existing questions about partner compatibility and evaluation units without establishing open-world failure or success for every LLM team. [EMAS-S034, pp.1–3; REQUAL-S057, arXiv:2611.06409v1, pp.1,3–5,7–8,10; EMAS-054,066–067,074.]

## 7. Robustness depends on what can fail

Adding replicas does not automatically add independent information. Ten agents repeating one wrong database entry preserve one evidential origin. Yet statistical independence is not necessary for every kind of resilience. Consider five deterministic replicas, at most two of which can send arbitrary messages. If the three correct replicas all compute the same message, a rule requiring three matching reports can reject two perfectly coordinated false reports. This finite construction does not rely on independent fault probabilities. If three replicas are corrupt, or the correct replicas share corrupted input, the stated protection no longer follows. The construction refutes the universal independence prerequisite while preserving the rejection of “decentralisation implies resilience.” [EMAS-005,048,058,075; author control RQ-CASE01; EMAS-S051.]

The FT-DCOP source provides a specialised version of this reasoning. Its utility-knowledge and replication conditions are defined per relevant set, with a bound on corrupt members, suitable correct knowledge and sufficient replicas. Its Max-Sum construction preserves the behaviour of the corresponding fault-free approximation under the model; it does not turn an approximate optimiser into an exact one. The native packet already recognised that latter limitation, so it is not presented as a newly found error. The supplementary proof is separately identified; reading it does not amount to an independent formal proof certificate. [EMAS-S051, Theorem 4.2, Proposition 5.1 and §6; EMAS-S051-supp; EMAS-075; formal F013.]

The newly explicit actuator boundary matters just as much as the replica bound. If a faulty computing agent can directly choose a different executed assignment, correct message reconstruction does not ensure the physical outcome. A truck that ignores its computed appointment time is outside a guarantee about reconstructing the optimiser’s messages. Reliable delivery, identifiable senders, deterministic correct computation and the knowledge model also constrain the construction. Thus the correction narrows applicability without rejecting the domain-specific mechanism. [EMAS-S051, §4.2; RQ-C03; author control RQ-CASE02.]

Other collective failures require different protections. Deadlock can arise from a cycle of individually reasonable waits; retrying every participant may increase contention. A participant may abandon an obligation without a replacement. Trust can be rebuilt strategically before another defection; the inherited con-man study does not prevent the first defection. Emergent conventions can produce collusion or exclusion. An explicit fault model, bounded exposure, progress measure and fallback are useful only to the extent that they cover the relevant cause. None licenses an open-ended claim of safety or legitimacy. [EMAS-055,058–064; EMAS-S016,040,049,051; native criticism and case registers.]

## 8. What the evidence can and cannot decide

Formal results, simulations, benchmarks, demonstrations and human experiments answer different questions. The exact 2023 debate preprint supplies positive evidence for its selected tasks, model copies and debate settings. It does not guarantee convergence to truth or establish superiority at every matched computation budget. A later negative comparison controls requested reasoning budgets more carefully, but requested tokens differ from tokens actually used, and its empirical result is conditional on tasks, models and architectures. Positive debate results and negative budget comparisons can therefore coexist without either becoming a universal MAS verdict. [EMAS-S048, pp.1,3–5,8; EMAS-S037, pp.3–7,9; EMAS-006,048–049,065–067.]

The native study already identified a mathematical overclaim in the latter paper’s strict information-loss argument. A representation can lose mutual information about a binary target while leaving the optimal classification decision and Bayes error unchanged. An explicit finite example in this candidate uses posterior probabilities 0.6 and 0.9; both favour the same class, and collapsing them preserves error 0.25 despite losing information. This does not refute the paper’s finite experiments or prove that compression never hurts. It blocks the general inference from strict information loss to strictly worse decision risk. The objection is preserved as inherited criticism, with a separately executed arithmetic control; it is not counted as a new native correction. [EMAS-K018; EMAS-S037, §3; RQ-CASE05.]

The alem evaluation uses a collection of environments, models and seeds, with normalised task rewards. Those are multiple observations within one study, not multiple independent replications. Its trained MARL references and zero-shot language agents have different training and inference budgets. Removing communication hurts selected coordination settings, while heterogeneous teams tend toward the performance of their constituent average rather than reliably reaching the strongest member. These are useful discriminators for mechanism choice, not proof that a model with a high aggregate score will coordinate reliably with arbitrary people. [EMAS-S038, pp.5–6,9 and §4.2.2; native EMP register.]

The human-explanation extended abstract is especially useful adverse evidence. It analysed 38 participants from 41 recruited, in groups of 14,14 and ten, over one layout and four 80-second sessions; participants were academically recruited and experienced with games. A nonsignificant difference in this small design is not evidence of equivalence, and an exploratory indication of faster adaptation is not a confirmed benefit. The correct next study would select a decision-relevant effect and adequate precision, vary tasks and recipients, and measure attention and performance together. This study neither establishes that explanation is useless nor requires an explanation layer everywhere. [EMAS-S054, complete extended abstract; EMAS-K024.]

Fresh source coverage is selective and declared. Twenty-six of the 56 native sources were revisited in exact primary-text scopes, ranging from selected pages to complete short papers. Thirty retain inherited primary-access assertions after their full native records and scientific uses were reviewed. A separate FT-DCOP supplement and one November preprint are additions to the requalification source register, not new native citation identities. Failed official FIPA retrievals and an unavailable HTML route remain recorded even where another route succeeded. This is predecessor-informed requalification, not independent replication or an exhaustive census of all publications through November. ## 9. Compose only the mechanisms the case needs

The six inherited configurations remain analytical possibilities, not validated whole systems. The following comparison makes their guards, burdens and reduction paths visible. Every detailed native participant and relation remains recoverable in the composition model.

| Configuration | Operative guard and contribution | Burden and omitted mechanisms | Reduction or retirement path |
|---|---|---|---|
| CFG01: direct coordination | Few participants, clear rights, simple dependency; establish who acts and observe its result | Messages and exception handling; no general auction, norm institution, learning or full plan theory | Use an ordinary service or one actor when it meets the same boundary. |
| CFG02: revisable cooperative team | Shared objective, partial plans and material notification; repair changes in contributions | Plan maintenance, communication and stale views; private teamwork does not supply strategic honesty | Return to direct coordination when uncertainty no longer justifies shared-plan machinery. |
| CFG03: open strategic interaction | Distinct principals and objectives; public obligations and selected allocation rules | Incentive, information, refusal, enforcement and dispute assumptions; full mutual belief is unavailable | Use posted offers or direct agreements if they satisfy consent and allocation needs. |
| CFG04: distributed optimisation | A decomposable objective plus an actual reason to distribute information or decisions | Width, memory, communication and leakage; privacy and Byzantine replication are optional separate extensions | Central or local optimisation when locality, privacy or ownership no longer justifies distribution. |
| CFG05: controlled adaptive collective | Repeated experience and testable populations; learning with explicit partner and cost checks | Training, evaluation, nonstationarity and transfer costs; CTDE and monotonic factorisation are choices | Fixed protocols when learning adds no robust gain or destabilises required constraints. |
| CFG06: human/institutional overlay | People or institutions hold material decisions or vetoes; actionable information and real powers | Attention, monitoring and legitimate authority; role labels and checkboxes are insufficient | Remove redundant structures while preserving the actual accountability and decision rights. |

Four inherited composition-induced candidates organise these tradeoffs. EMAS-071 concerns interaction closure: semantic interpretation, authority, obligation where relevant and observed consequences must connect. EMAS-072 concerns explicit handoffs between regimes with different assumptions, such as a cooperative interior and a strategic boundary. EMAS-073 concerns the minimum adequate configuration after accounting for total burden. EMAS-074 concerns adapting one component without silently breaking the dependencies of others. These are useful analytical obligations; their parents do not prove the whole composition empirically. [EMAS-071–074 and their native derivations, cases and alternatives.]

Removal tests keep their claims conditional. Removing institutional enforcement does not break a purely physical coordination task with no institutional claim; removing all ability to observe the relevant result does break a claim of verified interaction closure. CTDE is unnecessary for a fixed-policy team and is not required merely because evaluation occurs. A cost criterion is not an alternative algorithm to DPOP. Trust can change how much verification is worth paying for, but cannot replace the meaning of an obligation or make an unobserved result true. A local adaptation with no cross-participant dependency does not trigger the full dependency-aware adaptation burden. These distinctions motivate the six clarified relation rows while preserving their native identities and original qualifications. [EMAS-REL-010,032,036,037,046,055; `COMPOSITION_CHALLENGES.json`.]

The candidate claims no further composition-induced property. The added source on zero-shot coordination is subsumed by partner compatibility, evaluation units and dependency-aware adaptation; the actuator limitation narrows a specialised property rather than creating a new one. All negative cases, unresolved obligations and neutral conflicts remain part of the selected study. Removing them would make the positive mechanism list less accurate, not more mature.
